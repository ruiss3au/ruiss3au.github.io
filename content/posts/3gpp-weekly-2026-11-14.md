A shorter measurement interruption can cost more throughput than a longer, planned gap. When the network does not know that a receiver has stopped listening, the consequences can extend beyond the missing data: lost scheduling grants, unnecessary retransmissions and a lower transmission rate after the interruption. November evidence supports treating interruption timing as part of the scheduling design, alongside the duration of the interruption. [R4-2630391](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630391.zip), [R4-2631565](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2631565.zip).

That does not settle whether devices should ever choose their own interruption timing. Ericsson reports a favorable result for much shorter autonomous interruptions, while Apple argues that infrequent measurements deserve different treatment from frequently repeated blackouts. These are discussion papers for Radio Access Network Working Group 4 (RAN4) in Calgary, 16-20 November. They support a conditional engineering argument, not a new adopted requirement. [R4-2631565](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2631565.zip), [R4-2630453](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630453.zip).

## The baseline is a scheduler that knows when to wait

A measurement gap gives the network a known interval in which a device is unavailable for normal scheduling. Gapless measurement can still involve short interruptions while the device retunes its radio. The distinction is whether the scheduler knows when those interruptions happen. Qualcomm separates device-autonomous interruptions, interruptions known to both network and device, and measurements that cause no interruption at all. Removing a configured gap therefore does not necessarily produce uninterrupted reception. [R4-2611989](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_120/Docs/R4-2611989.zip), [R4-2632068](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2632068.zip).

The problem was already visible in Nokia's August contribution from Maastricht. Its autonomous model imposed two one-millisecond blackouts every 40 milliseconds while leaving the device in the scheduler. The paper identified an additional loss when link adaptation mistook interruption-related failures for worse radio conditions. Network-visible timing was already Nokia's preferred direction; that position is earlier context, rather than a November discovery. [R4-2611989](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_120/Docs/R4-2611989.zip).

The November contribution makes the dependency more explicit. It includes losses of control, data and retransmission feedback, compares protected and unprotected control channels, and separates ordinary link adaptation from an idealized version that does not react incorrectly to interruptions. The comparison tests which losses disappear when one part of the procedure is protected. [R4-2630391](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630391.zip).

## One blackout can affect three transmission opportunities

Nokia's updated system model uses two half-millisecond interruptions around each measurement occasion, repeated every 40 milliseconds. Their combined duration occupies 2.5% of the time. In its unrestricted autonomous case, labeled S1-O1, the interruption may affect the control channel as well as data and feedback. Nokia describes this as an almost worst-case model using scheduling grants for a later slot. [R4-2630391](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630391.zip).

Consider an interruption in one slot. It can erase the data being received then, prevent the device from acknowledging an earlier reception, and erase the grant needed for a subsequent reception. Nokia models up to three affected transport-block transmission opportunities through these dependencies. A transport block is the data unit whose reception and retransmission are being tracked here. The interruption need not physically last three slots to damage three opportunities. [R4-2630391](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630391.zip).

Protecting the control channel removes one route for that damage. In S1-O2, Nokia assumes control reception survives, subject to agreement with the physical-layer group. Current data and feedback for earlier data can still be lost, leaving up to two affected transmission opportunities under the same assumptions. Control protection is therefore useful, but it does not protect the complete transaction. [R4-2630391](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630391.zip).

The second amplification mechanism operates through outer-loop link adaptation, the network's adjustment of transmission settings using reception feedback. A modulation and coding scheme (MCS) determines how data is represented and protected. In Nokia's model, missing or unreliable feedback can drive a more conservative MCS even though the radio channel has not deteriorated. The resulting loss continues through the selected transmission settings, beyond the resources directly interrupted. [R4-2630391](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630391.zip).

These mechanisms explain why an interruption ratio is not a throughput-loss ceiling. One measures unavailable time. The other includes failed dependent transmissions and the scheduler's response to their feedback. A small ratio can still produce a much larger penalty when the network treats an unavailable receiver as a receiver experiencing poor radio conditions. [R4-2611989](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_120/Docs/R4-2611989.zip), [R4-2630391](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630391.zip).

The comparison isolates two improvements. Protecting control reception reduces loss under either adaptation model. Suppressing misleading feedback reactions also helps, recovering roughly 15 percentage points in the unrestricted case. Yet the ideal result still loses 23.4%, and the protected-control result still loses 18.1%. Correcting the adaptation error alone does not bring loss close to the raw interruption ratio in this experiment. [R4-2630391](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630391.zip).

This qualifies the earlier baseline. Nokia's August discussion described ideal adaptation as almost aligning throughput with uninterrupted operation. The November model explicitly includes more control and feedback dependencies and retains a substantial residual under ideal adaptation. These are different experiments, so their percentages cannot establish a measured trend. They do show why a mitigation demonstrated against one loss mechanism must be tested again when the surrounding procedure is modeled more completely. [R4-2611989](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_120/Docs/R4-2611989.zip), [R4-2630391](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630391.zip).

## Shorter interruptions supply a real counterexample

Ericsson's full-buffer evaluation uses 30 devices and an 80-millisecond repetition period. With half-millisecond visible interruptions, its network-controlled option, S2-O1, loses less than 0.5% relative to uninterrupted operation. Its autonomous half-millisecond option delivers throughput comparable to a six-millisecond measurement gap, despite occupying much less nominal time. That supports the concern that shorter unannounced blackouts can surrender their apparent airtime advantage. [R4-2631565](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2631565.zip).

But the same full-buffer discussion reports that a six-symbol autonomous interruption achieves throughput comparable to the network-controlled option. Symbols provide a finer interruption duration than whole slots. This result prevents a blanket conclusion that network-visible timing must always outperform autonomous operation by a large margin: sufficiently short interruptions can change the comparison under the evaluated conditions. [R4-2631565](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2631565.zip).

Short duration alone is not a guarantee that the interrupted data survives. Nokia's separate link-level experiment deliberately avoids the control and channel-estimation symbols. At a signal-to-noise ratio of 20 decibels, it nevertheless reports almost complete decoding failure for interrupted blocks at MCS index 13 and above with a single-symbol interruption. This is a result for the affected blocks under that experiment's coding and receiver assumptions, not an average failure rate for all traffic. [R4-2630391](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630391.zip).

The Ericsson and Nokia results answer different parts of the problem. One reports system throughput with a short-interruption model; the other tests whether damaged blocks can still be decoded at particular transmission settings. Different device loads and repetition periods also separate their system experiments. The extracted parameter tables do not permit a matched receiver-and-scheduler comparison. Consequently, these results support testing short autonomy, while withholding a general claim that preserving most symbols preserves most throughput. [R4-2631565](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2631565.zip), [R4-2630391](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630391.zip).

## Infrequent autonomy makes a different bargain

Apple's case is narrower than allowing a receiver to disappear arbitrarily. It proposes network-authorized autonomous measurements, with interruptions associated with configured measurement occasions and bounded in both length and frequency. The network would know the candidate occasions, but not which subset the device actually uses. This preserves flexibility when measurements compete inside the device. [R4-2630453](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630453.zip).

Repetition changes the stakes. Apple contrasts half-millisecond interruptions before and after an occasion repeated every 640 milliseconds, about 0.16% interruption time, with the same interruptions every 40 milliseconds, 2.5%. It argues that the maximum interruption ratio and individual interruption length should be considered together with the applicable measurement scenarios. A result from frequently repeated interruptions cannot, by itself, establish that every sparse autonomous use is unacceptable. [R4-2630453](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630453.zip).

Apple also explains what generic control-channel protection trades away. With multiple carriers received at different times, the device must adjust radio switching to avoid each carrier's control reception. Protecting control alone still leaves dependent data and feedback vulnerable. Apple therefore favors bounded autonomy as a baseline and limits its suggested applicability for control-protected autonomy to synchronized cases. Nokia's measured benefit from control protection and Apple's implementation objection can both hold: a useful protection mechanism may still be too costly to require across every timing arrangement. [R4-2630453](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630453.zip), [R4-2630391](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630391.zip).

Qualcomm adds a system-level qualification to the shorter-symbol argument. Preserving extra symbols for one device assumes it would otherwise have been scheduled in them. Under partial traffic load, the benefit depends on scheduling probability; the network may instead serve another device in an unavailable slot. Qualcomm also identifies the need for precise interruption timing and more complex scheduling within a slot, and proposes starting with slot-level granularity while reconsidering finer timing if its benefit warrants that complexity. [R4-2632068](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2632068.zip).

Read together, the evidence separates three conditions that an interruption-time percentage collapses: whether the network knows the blackout, whether the affected transmission can survive it, and whether the saved resources would carry additional useful traffic. Nokia demonstrates the cost of opaque timing and damaged feedback; Ericsson supplies a favorable shorter-interruption case; Apple and Qualcomm explain why repetition, timing alignment and traffic determine its applicability. The conditional inference is that autonomous operation needs an operating envelope, rather than a throughput promise derived from its interruption ratio. [R4-2630391](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630391.zip), [R4-2631565](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2631565.zip), [R4-2630453](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2630453.zip), [R4-2632068](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG4_Radio/TSGR4_121/Docs/R4-2632068.zip).

The decisive next comparison should hold measurement repetition, traffic load and radio conditions constant, then test known and autonomous timing at both slot and symbol durations. It should retain realistic grant and acknowledgement dependencies, state how interrupted symbols enter decoding, and compare ordinary feedback handling with any implementable mitigation. Repeating that test for sparse measurements and differently timed carriers would establish where bounded autonomy earns its flexibility, and where a network-known interruption remains the better scheduling contract.
