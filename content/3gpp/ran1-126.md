Maastricht, 24-28 August 2026, brought a practical question into focus: how much
extra signaling, receiver processing, and implementation complexity should 6G
accept in exchange for better spectral efficiency and lower energy consumption?
The late meeting summaries make that question more concrete. They record a
tracking reference-signal baseline, refine the experiments for downlink control
and channel-state information (CSI) feedback, and identify work to carry into October. The
modulation discussion also contains a real disagreement over whether shaping
gains survive realistic receiver and channel assumptions.

The clearest account of this meeting starts with the machinery of the radio.
A network can save transmissions by sleeping, shorten control messages by
splitting them, or improve its channel estimate by combining uplink and
downlink observations. Each choice creates another coordination problem
between the network and the device. The final summaries document progress in
defining those problems, with several agreements explicitly about what to
study rather than what to implement
[R1-2606925](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606925.zip),
[R1-2606931](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606931.zip).

## Modulation: the gain has to survive the receiver

### The coalition for optional shaping

Constellation shaping was one of the sharper decisions facing the 6G study.
Uniform quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM) uses a regular set of constellation
points with equal probabilities. Probabilistic shaping changes how frequently
points are used; geometric shaping changes where they sit. A useful gain must
survive coding, channel estimation, multiple antenna layers, power-amplifier
constraints, and the network's ability to choose the right transmission mode.
The moderator summary treats those system questions as part of the decision,
including retransmission behavior and channel-state feedback
[R1-2606883](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606883.zip).

A joint contribution from T-Mobile USA, Charter, CableLabs, Rogers, Reliance
Jio, Far EasTone, and partners including Qualcomm, MediaTek, Lenovo and DeepSig
proposes probabilistic shaping as an optional user equipment (UE) feature configurable by the
network. Its argument is that gains extend across mobile broadband, fixed
wireless access, and line-of-sight satellite scenarios, while the square QAM
lattice permits reuse of receiver machinery. It leaves a choice between
enumerative sphere shaping (ESS) and an error-correcting-code-based distribution
matcher, and between one-bit and two-bit shaping
[R1-2606409](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606409.zip).

The timing matters. That contribution reproduces the RAN#112 direction to
decide in the third quarter whether to support shaping and to stop the study
if consensus is absent. Optional support is the coalition's proposed way to
make the feature usable without requiring every device to implement it. It
does not remove the need to define compatible transmitter and receiver
behavior, or prove that the feature earns its complexity.

### Apple challenges the operating assumptions

Apple's contribution tests precisely those costs. It reports losses for
ESS-based probabilistic amplitude shaping against uniform QAM with an updated
modulation and coding scheme (MCS) table in several closed-loop multiple-input
multiple-output (MIMO) configurations, and mixed results in
others. In its uplink discrete-Fourier-transform-spread orthogonal frequency
division multiplexing (DFT-s-OFDM) evaluation, power backoff can erase the
expected benefit. These are Apple's results under its stated assumptions,
not universal performance limits
[R1-2606020](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606020.zip).

Its more consequential objection concerns adaptation. Neither endpoint has
perfect advance knowledge of whether shaped or uniform modulation will work
better. Frequency selectivity, inter-cell interference, unequal spatial
layers, and implementation-specific receivers can all change the answer.
Apple also identifies consequences for interleaving, transport-block size
calculation, and finite-precision processing. The dispute is about the whole
link adaptation procedure as well as the constellation.

The moderator's later summary preserves alternatives for geometric and
probabilistic shaping alongside an alternative in which neither enhancement
is needed. It explicitly leaves continued study conditional. It also records
disagreement about whether some additional design choices are necessary.
That supports reporting an unresolved adoption question; it does not support
declaring a shaping winner at Maastricht. The next checkpoint needs a
comparison that includes overhead, retransmissions and receiver assumptions,
not merely the best gain from a favorable channel
[R1-2606883](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606883.zip).

## Control signaling: a better defined experiment

The final CMCC/Apple moderator summary contains a clearly labeled RAN1#126
agreement section. It sharpens the study of two-stage downlink control
information, or DCI. One stage can tell the UE how to obtain another, allowing
the frequently monitored part of control signaling to be smaller. The
tradeoff is that successful scheduling may depend on decoding both stages
and observing the timing relationship between them
[R1-2606925](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606925.zip).

For a physical downlink control channel (PDCCH) second stage, the study can consider the same or different
control-resource sets. A modified second-stage PDCCH may have additional
aggregation levels, more flexible placement, or higher-order modulation.
Another alternative uses a physical downlink shared channel (PDSCH) transmission scheme with polar coding,
quadrature phase-shift keying (QPSK) and single-layer transmission as its starting restrictions. This is a
set of alternatives for evaluation, not an adopted two-stage physical channel.

The agreed comparison includes single-stage DCI payloads of 40, 60 and 140
bits, excluding the cyclic redundancy check (CRC), with a default 24-bit CRC. The summary also lays out
two-stage payload assumptions. These common starting points matter because
a comparison between different payload budgets can make a control design
look better for reasons unrelated to its structure.

RAN1 also agreed to study one DCI scheduling multiple shared-channel
transmissions across slots on a single carrier, with separate transport
blocks. That could amortize signaling, but limits on monitoring sizes,
padding, truncation and behavior during bandwidth adaptation remain open.
Fallback DCI is constrained to avoid dependence on UE-specific radio resource
control (RRC) configuration. The outstanding design problem is how to offer flexible
scheduling while keeping the discovery and decoding burden predictable for
the UE. These explicit boundaries are a more useful outcome than a claim
that the new control channel has been finalized.

## CSI and tracking: firmer baselines, unfinished choices

### A tracking signal beyond the synchronization block

The final Lenovo moderator summary records support for a single-port
reference signal, in addition to the synchronization signal block, as a
baseline for finer time and frequency tracking in connected mode. Multi-port
tracking remains available for further study. This is a concrete Maastricht
baseline, and its scope matters: it does not settle every signal pattern,
periodicity or operating state
[R1-2606931](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606931.zip).

Tracking lets the receiver follow timing and frequency changes between the
coarser synchronization opportunities. It becomes particularly relevant when
the network reduces always-on transmissions. The same summary explicitly
targets RAN1#126bis for an agreement on the proposed use of both periodic and
aperiodic tracking signals. Candidate periodicities include 20, 40 and 80 ms;
the role of data or control demodulation reference signals in aperiodic
tracking remains a choice under study. Those values are candidates in the
carried-forward proposal, not settled deployment settings.

This leaves October with an identifiable engineering decision: how much
regular tracking support is necessary, and when can on-demand transmissions
fill the gap? The evaluation must account for reference-signal overhead
across multiple transmission points, multiple carriers and wide bandwidths.

### Combining uplink and downlink evidence

The same final summary agrees evaluation baselines for joint uplink/downlink
CSI acquisition. In one use case, a combined scheme must be compared with
uplink sounding reference signals alone and downlink CSI reference signals
alone. The additional evaluation criteria include user-perceived throughput,
reference-signal and feedback overhead, computation, model complexity and
storage. This prevents a richer channel estimate from being judged without
counting what it costs to obtain it
[R1-2606931](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606931.zip).

The downlink CSI summary separately records study of a low-resolution W1W2
codebook structure, initially considering ranks one through four. It uses an
oversampled two-dimensional DFT basis as a baseline and keeps several choices
about basis selection, co-phasing and higher ranks open. It also explicitly
requires attention to the UE's search complexity. The outcome is a narrower
design space, not a finished codebook
[R1-2606903](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606903.zip).

Beam management has related dependencies. The ZTE moderator summary covers
network-initiated measurements and indications alongside UE-initiated events,
and points preparation toward RAN1#126bis. Its extracted historical agreement
section includes the distinction between a deteriorating serving beam and a
candidate beam becoming better. Those event definitions belong to earlier
meetings in the summary; they should not be relabeled as August decisions.
The current report's useful forward question is how event detection, an
uplink report, the network response and beam application fit together without
excessive latency
[R1-2606933](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606933.zip).

## Energy saving: who pays to wake the network?

Uplink wake-up signaling makes the dependency especially clear. A sleeping
cell can save network energy, but the UE still needs enough information to
send a detectable request at an appropriate time, frequency, power and beam.
The LG Electronics moderator summary records Maastricht study agreements
covering alternative ways to obtain the wake-up configuration: prior
configuration, synchronization signals, the physical broadcast channel (PBCH), broadcast PDSCH and common
PDCCH. Their costs include signaling payload, UE complexity and energy at
both endpoints
[R1-2606898](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606898.zip).

For non-colocated cells, the same summary identifies further work on using
predefined transmit power, mitigating interference, and using information
from another cell to estimate path loss. A broad transmit beam may help a
UE reach a sleeping receiver but reduce coverage compared with a narrow beam.
This makes deployment geometry part of the energy-saving design. Saving a
base-station transmission does not automatically reduce total energy if
devices must transmit more aggressively to find it.

Downlink wake-up signals raise a complementary issue: how much additional
information can be placed in a low-energy receiver's path before that path
becomes too complicated? The Apple moderator summary records common
assessment criteria covering coverage, false detection and missed detection,
resource overhead, latency, energy at the UE and network, and overlap with
existing mechanisms. It also records study of additional idle/inactive
functions, including system-information change notifications and triggering
downlink small-data transmission where supported
[R1-2606858](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606858.zip).

Apple's own contribution proposes coordinating wake-up signaling with cell
discontinuous transmission/reception and UE discontinuous reception. Its
two-level proposal distinguishes waking a cell active period from waking a
particular UE. That is a company proposal, separate from the moderator's
recorded assessment criteria
[R1-2606039](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606039.zip).

## Bandwidth: flexibility before the device is known

Initial access creates another tension between flexibility and a common
baseline. NTT DOCOMO argues for a simpler bandwidth-part mechanism with only
essential configuration, and proposes multiple candidate initial bandwidth
setups to accommodate different UE types. It also asks to study where
bandwidth-dependent operation can begin during random access
[R1-2606503](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606503.zip).

MediaTek questions the need for elaborate early capability differentiation
in narrow low-frequency deployments. It warns that additional broadcast
configuration can cost synchronization coverage and that fragmenting initial
uplink resources complicates access. Its preferred starting point is a
realistic capability-independent baseline, with additional mechanisms
justified quantitatively
[R1-2606145](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606145.zip).

The bandwidth moderator summary still marks relevant choices as open or on
hold: when uplink and downlink center frequencies may differ, what UE
capability is required, and when bandwidth adaptation can begin during
connection establishment. These are consequential choices because the
network initially knows less about the device than it does after capability
exchange. An early optimization has to preserve a usable access path for
devices that cannot take it
[R1-2606837](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606837.zip).

Multiple-carrier operation adds a related dispute. The NTT DOCOMO moderator
summary reports interest in studying both a Gothia-cell-like approach and a
second aggregation method, while recording Ericsson's request to establish
the additional motivation for the former. That is evidence of the discussion
and its unresolved rationale, not proof that one architecture displaced the
other. The next useful contribution would connect an architectural choice
to a measured benefit in control overhead, scheduling or deployment
[R1-2606886](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606886.zip).

## Satellite access when location becomes unreliable

The satellite work has a more immediate procedural problem: deciding what a
device may do when its global navigation satellite system (GNSS) position
becomes unreliable. Satellite motion creates timing and frequency offsets;
reusing a stale position can leave uplink compensation wrong even while the
UE continues receiving the network. The incoming RAN4 analysis identifies
requirements work that can feed the RAN1 study report. Its extracted impact
table is incomplete, so detailed limits cannot be recovered from that liaison
[R1-2605189](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2605189.zip).

CATT asks RAN4 to distinguish a GNSS-less UE from one with degraded GNSS,
including a threshold, random-access timing and frequency tolerances, and
test cases. It proposes a detection requirement for the new physical random
access channel (PRACH) sequence while reusing the legacy physical uplink
shared channel (PUSCH) demodulation requirement
[R1-2605706](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2605706.zip).

HONOR proposes a network-configured validity duration for the last GNSS
position, with a possible network-authorized extension. It offers reference
location selection using either the last known position or measured reference
signals, and proposes reporting when GNSS accuracy degrades or becomes
invalid. Its random-access proposals also address which occasion identifies
the response when two PRACH sequences occupy different occasions
[R1-2606185](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606185.zip).

These contributions expose complementary pieces of the same problem: a
testable classification of the UE's condition and a signaling procedure that
keeps network and device synchronized about it. They do not establish a
new August agreement on those choices. The next evidence to seek is a
common validity rule, compensation behavior and matching test assumptions.
That work also explains why GNSS-dependent terrestrial-to-satellite handover
and GNSS-resilient satellite access must be tracked separately.

## What left the meeting, and what comes next

Two outgoing liaisons establish concrete handoffs. RAN1 sent preliminary
multi-radio-access-technology spectrum sharing (MRSS) performance assessments to the plenary, covering
frequency-division duplex (FDD) and time-division duplex (TDD) under different overhead assumptions. The results are in an
attachment that is not extracted locally, so the liaison supports reporting
the handoff but no particular performance gain or final MRSS conclusion
[R1-2606926](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606926.zip).

For Rel-20 artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) air-interface work, RAN1 also informed RAN2 of an agreement
on Layer 3 (L3) signaling to collect paired target-CSI and CSI-feedback samples for
performance monitoring. The prose establishes that interface request; the
missing embedded agreement details prevent a claim about the exact protocol
format. The next step is RAN2's treatment of the request
[R1-2606930](https://www.3gpp.org/ftp/tsg_ran/WG1_RL1/TSGR1_126/Docs/R1-2606930.zip).

The meeting's outgoing liaisons list RAN1#126bis in Jeju on 12-16 October and
RAN1#127 in Calgary on 16-20 November. The most specific October target in
the reviewed final summaries is the periodic/aperiodic tracking-signal
decision. Control signaling, CSI complexity and wake-up feasibility provide
the next tests of whether the study's extra flexibility can be made
practical. Shaping has a separate third-quarter decision pressure in the
reproduced plenary direction; it should not silently be assumed to continue
through October.

Satellite readers should pair this report with the
[RAN4#120 report](/3gpp/ran4-120/). SpaceX's RAN1 contribution on 6G non-terrestrial network (NTN)
requirements remains unavailable as extracted text and supplies no technical
position for this account. The strongest verified satellite-terrestrial
disputes in the current material are in RAN4's coexistence and handover work.
